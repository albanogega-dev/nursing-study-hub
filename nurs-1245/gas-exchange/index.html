Gas Exchange

Gas exchange is the intake of oxygen and release of carbon dioxide. It is made possible by respiration and perfusion and occurs by diffusion between air and blood.

Tap a step above.

Diffusion

HIGH → LOW

O₂: alveoli → capillary blood
CO₂: capillary blood → alveoli

Control of respiration

  • Chemoreceptors: medulla; respond to H+ and PaCO₂.
  • Mechanical receptors: upper conducting airways; respond to irritants, muscle stretching, alveolar wall distortion; initiate cough reflex and aid respiratory control.

Factors affecting respiratory function

  • Acute/chronic disease
  • Activity level
  • Environmental pollutants
  • Smoking/exposure
  • Medications
  • Psychological anxiety

Developmental / age considerations

  • Infants: short airways; aspiration risk.
  • Children: eustachian tubes, bronchi and bronchioles elongated/less angular; frequent colds/infections.
  • Older adults: stiffer lung tissues, ↓ muscle strength, skeletal alterations, digestive changes.
ABCs always take priority.

Respiratory complications

Hypoxia: inadequate oxygen available to cells.

Hypoventilation: decreased rate or depth of air movement into lungs.

Hyperventilation: increased rate and depth of ventilation above normal requirements.

Dyspnea: listed as a major respiratory complication.

Hypoxia

  • Restlessness = early sign
  • Altered LOC
  • Dizziness
  • Behavioral changes
  • PO₂ <80 mmHg

Noninvasive diagnostics

  • Pulse oximetry
  • Pulmonary function studies: TV, VC, FVC, FEV, TLC, RV, PEFR
  • Chest X-ray — compare
  • Sputum analysis: culture/cytology; order required

Pulse oximetry

Noninvasive measurement of arterial oxygen saturation. Probe: finger, toe, earlobe, bridge of nose.

Normal: 95–99%

Slide notes: 90% is important to support life; treat if <92%; COPD 88%; know hemoglobin; trend values; ABGs may be needed.

Peak flow meter

  1. Sit/stand straight.
  2. Indicator to base.
  3. Deep breath.
  4. Seal lips around mouthpiece.
  5. Blow hard and fast — one quick blow.
  6. Repeat twice more.
  7. Use highest of 3 efforts.
  8. Record result.

Respiratory interventions

Health promotion/patient education • breathing techniques • incentive spirometer • chest physiotherapy • suctioning • comfort • medications • oxygen therapy • airway adjuncts • chest tubes • artificial airway management.

Proper breathing techniques

  • Deep breathing
  • Cough
  • Incentive spirometer
  • Pursed-lip breathing
  • Sitting / walking
  • Abdominal breathing

Cough medications

Expectorant: guaifenesin (Robitussin)

Suppressants/antitussives: codeine, dextromethorphan (Robitussin DM)

Local anesthetic: lozenges

Incentive spirometry

  • Post-op periods
  • Chest trauma
  • Keeps alveoli from collapsing
  • Reinforces deep breathing
  • Promotes cough
  • Maximizes lung inflation
  • Reduces atelectasis
  • Promotes optimal gas exchange

Chest physiotherapy

Percussion/vibration loosens pulmonary secretions. Slide notes: 30–60 seconds over an area, 3 times/day; assess response.

Comfort

Semi-high positioning • adequate fluids 2–3 L/24 hr • humidified air.

MDI

  • Shake canister
  • Hold upright
  • Inhale through mouth slowly
  • One spray with one breath
  • Hold breath afterward

Spacer

Decreases particle velocity/large particles, improves delivery. One-way valve reduces coordination requirement. Face mask may be used when mouthpiece cannot be used.

DPI / Nebulizer

DPI: breath activated; maintain upright; follow package instructions.

Nebulizer: useful for aerosolized solution meds, acute distress/reduced inspiratory flow, or patients unable to coordinate MDI/DPI technique.

Oxygen therapy goals

PaO₂ 80–100 mmHg • PaCO₂ <45 mmHg

Different criteria may be used for COPD.

Benefits

  • ↑ O₂ availability
  • ↓ work of breathing
  • ↓ work of heart/vascular system
  • ↓ associated fatigue

Risks / hazards

  • Pulmonary tissue damage
  • Impaired stimulus to breathe
  • Retinal vasoconstriction
  • Combustion
  • Infection
  • Drying/irritation

Desired response

Normal HR/rhythm • normal RR • alert/oriented • normal color. Treatment should improve tachycardia, arrhythmia, increased RR, confusion and cyanosis.

Combustion prevention

No smoking • grounded electrical equipment/outlets • no frayed cords/extension cords • check electrical equipment • avoid oily hair products, grease and flammable solutions.

Oxygen delivery devices

Nasal Cannula

Room air 21%; 1 L=24%, 2 L=28%, 3 L=32%, 4 L=36%, 5 L=40%, 6 L=44%.

Not used above 6 LPM in slide. Can eat. Concerns: dryness, dislodging, mouth breathing; complete nasal blockage; pressure damage.

High Flow

Slide lists 10 L/min≈65%, 15 L/min≈90%; aerosolized; best tolerated by children.

Simple Face Mask

Slides list 35–60% at 6–10 L/min and elsewhere 40–60% at 5–10 L/min. Never less than 5 LPM.

Short-term use; cannot eat; close fit; breakdown/hypercapnia concerns.

Partial Rebreather

6–15 LPM = 70–90%. Reservoir collects first exhaled air; about 1/3 expired air rebreathed. Bag should only partially deflate.

Tight seal; watch kinks/twists; hypercapnia, oxygen deprivation, skin breakdown; cannot eat.

Nonrebreather

Highest O₂ concentration with a mask; up to 100%; slide lists 70–100%, flow 15 L/min. One-way valves; reservoir filled with O₂.

Temporary high-O₂ use; suffocation risk if malfunction; cannot eat; slide states not for CO₂ retention.

Venturi Mask

Most precise oxygen concentration. Fixed concentrations: 24%, 28%, 35%, 40%. Flow usually 4–10 L.

Adjust dial/orifice; watch vents — occlusion can make O₂ too high.

Mask concerns / nursing care

Claustrophobia, hot/confining, difficult to keep in place, tight seal, skin breakdown, eating/talking difficulty. Clean face/mask secretions, support claustrophobia, check/pad pressure areas, request nasal cannula at meals when possible.

O₂ therapy nursing interventions & weaning

Assess respirations, pulse ox, skin, mucous membranes; know how to convert devices; hygiene to face/nares/mask/pressure areas. Wean to lower concentration and reassess HR, RR and pulse ox.

Artificial airways

  • Nasopharyngeal airway
  • Oropharyngeal airway — keeps tongue out of airway, allows suctioning, may be used until consciousness returns post-op.
  • Endotracheal tube — inserted via nose/mouth into trachea.
  • Tracheostomy — artificial opening into trachea.

Purpose of tracheostomy

Prolonged mechanical ventilation/delayed wean • inability to manage tracheobronchial secretions • sleep apnea • upper airway obstruction/trauma/mass.

Uncuffed

Advantages: free airflow around tube/larynx, less tracheal damage risk.

Disadvantages: ↑ aspiration risk; adapter needed for ventilation.

Cuffed

Prevents air leaks during positive-pressure ventilation; blocks aspiration; even pressure distribution; cannot speak when inflated.

Fenestrated / speaking

Speech possible through upper airway when capped and cuff deflated; mechanical ventilation possible with inner cannula and cuff inflated; possible fenestration occlusion/inner cannula dislodgment.

Trach sizing

Slides note Jackson sizing for Shiley regular/Flex; ISO used by other manufacturers and Shiley XLT. ISO size refers to inner diameter without inner cannula.

Tracheostomy care

  • Clean inner cannula using sterile technique
  • Clean insertion-site skin; monitor breakdown
  • No cotton-type dressings
  • Support communication
  • Ensure tube security
  • Obturator at bedside
  • Spare trach tube at bedside
  • Trach shield provides O₂ or humidified room air

Suctioning

Used to clear tracheobronchial secretions. Method depends on LOC, cooperation and artificial airway availability.

Before suction: visual assessment, auscultation for rales/rhonchi/wheezes, tactile assessment/fremitus.

Risks: infection, arrhythmias/dysrhythmias, hypoxia/hypoxemia, mucosal trauma, atelectasis, death.

Slide emphasis: often hyperoxygenate before suctioning; assess color, heart rate, secretions (color, amount, consistency); sterile technique; proper insertion length/protocol; sterile water; assess breath sounds.

Nursing diagnosis examples from slides

RC: Atelectasis

Ineffective Breathing Pattern related to excessive/thick secretions secondary to pneumonia AEB RR=32 and oxygen saturation=88%.

Outcome: atelectasis

Patient will improve atelectasis by end of shift AEB:

  • Lung sounds with less rhonchi
  • No complaints of dyspnea

Outcome: breathing pattern

Patient will have improved breathing pattern by end of shift AEB:

  • RR 18–22
  • O₂ saturation >92%

Documentation

  • Before/after assessments: site, pain, lung sounds, oxygen saturation; skin breakdown from trach collar; care given.
  • Suctioning: time, pre/post assessment, reason, secretion characteristics and amount.
  • O₂ mask/device: type, amount of oxygen, saturation, lung sounds, respiratory rate/pattern; pre/post assessment.
  • Document O₂ amount applied, RR, O₂ saturation and lung sounds before/after intervention.
